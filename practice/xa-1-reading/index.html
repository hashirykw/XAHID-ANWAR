Section A: Comprehension and Use of Language

Text A: The Shop on Empress Market Road

My grandfather's bicycle shop was barely wider than its own doorway. Every morning at seven he rolled up the shutter with a noise like a train braking, and the smell of rubber and old oil drifted out onto the pavement to greet the first customers of the day.

I spent the summer I turned fourteen sitting on an upturned crate behind his counter. I had been sent there, my mother said, "to learn something useful", though I suspected it was really to keep me away from my phone. For the first week I learned nothing except how slowly a clock can move.

Nana Abu did not explain things. He simply handed me tools. A spanner arrived in my palm, then a tyre lever, then a tin of grease so thick it looked like honey that had given up. When I fumbled, he said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than shouting.

It was a schoolboy who changed everything. He came in near closing time, pushing a bicycle with a front wheel bent into the shape of a crisp. His shirt was torn and his eyes were fixed on the floor. "My father will see it," he whispered, as if the bicycle might tell on him.

Nana Abu glanced at the clock, then at me. "Your wheel," he said, and walked into the back room.

I worked for an hour. My fingers were black and my back ached, but when the boy rode away, the wheel turned true and silent beneath him. He looked back over his shoulder twice. Behind me, I heard my grandfather put the kettle on, humming a tune I had never once heard him hum.

Section B: Summary and Short Response

Text B: Plastic on Our Beaches

Visitors to Clifton and Sea View often notice the bags and bottles before they notice the waves. Much of this waste is not dropped on the beach at all. It is washed down the city's storm drains and nullahs during the monsoon, carrying rubbish from markets and streets many kilometres inland.

Single-use plastic is the biggest problem. Shopping bags, water bottles and food wrappers are cheap, light and easily blown away, and few of them are ever collected for recycling. On busy weekends, visitors add to the pile, leaving snack packets behind because there are too few bins, and the bins that exist are emptied irregularly.

Some groups are fighting back. Volunteer clean-ups now collect hundreds of kilograms of waste in a single morning. A number of schools run awareness weeks, and some shopkeepers near the beach have started charging for plastic bags, which has noticeably reduced how many are handed out. Researchers argue that the most lasting change would come from fitting nets at the mouths of storm drains, so that rubbish is caught before it ever reaches the sea.
